Cognitive and neural models of

human learning

I am broadly interested in the flexibility of human learning. My early work focused on how the sensorimotor system operates under multiple sources of uncertainty to support efficient adaptation; I am now extending this to how we plan movements and make decisions when interacting with other agents.

Approach

The motor system operates under constant noise and uncertainty, yet it still produces accurate, generalizable movement. I study how it manages this across two distinct systems — implicit recalibration and action selection — using behavioral experiments paired with computational models.

I also work on the cerebellum, a structure long tied to motor control, and its role in broader cognitive domains: timing, intuitive physics, and theory of mind. This work combines modeling with neurophysiological and behavioral studies in cerebellar ataxia patients.

Theme 01

How the brain adapts to uncertainties in the environment

Our motor system must deal with enormous amounts of noise and uncertainty. Our visual and proprioceptive inputs are imperfect, and both the internal state of the body and the external environment are constantly changing. It is impressive that the motor system can still perform accurate movements and generalize skills despite these uncertainties — something that remains a major challenge for robotic systems.

We examine how the motor system adapts to uncertainty across two distinct systems: the implicit recalibration system and the action selection system. The recalibration system corrects for small errors automatically and does not require attentional resources. In contrast, the action selection system is more flexible and can adjust behavior to meet specific task demands.

Our recent work suggests that implicit recalibration is insensitive to state noise, yet shows unique effects in response to contextual uncertainty: learning is faster but less durable when the environment is volatile, reversal learning is slower than original learning, and relearning is slower rather than faster on re-exposure. We proposed a Cerebellar Population Coding (CPC) model that explains these contextual effects without invoking contextual inference — a simple two-layer network corresponding to cerebellar cortex and deep cerebellar nuclei, in which contextual effects emerge from population activation.

To investigate action selection under uncertainty, we developed a novel air-hockey game requiring multi-dimensional motor control and compensation for time-varying wind. The action selection system showed near-optimal modulation, where state noise and volatility had opposite effects on learning rate. Participants performed significantly worse on a non-motor version of the same task.

Beyond the physical world, another key source of uncertainty comes from other agents. Incorporating it requires theory of mind. My current studies focus on how we make decisions in the face of uncertain intentions from other agents.

Theme 02

Movement biases reveal fundamental computations in motor control

Biases in movement offer a window into the control mechanisms underlying motor behavior. We focus on biases across three time scales: stable target-specific biases, biases induced by prior distributions, and trial-by-trial biases from recent movements. We have developed novel models to explain the origins of each.

Our work on target-specific bias shows that the pattern reflects a mismatch between the proprioceptive space that encodes hand position and the visual space used for target localization. Modeling suggests participants perceive the target visually, translate it into proprioceptive space, and then generate a motor plan.

Prior distributions also induce central tendency effects in reaching. Contrary to prevailing beliefs, we find this bias to be largely non-Bayesian: while motor bias is modulated by the prior, motor variance is not. Ongoing work focuses on the computational basis of this discrepancy.

We also identified a novel trial-by-trial bias in which the current movement is biased away from the previous one. The pattern and modulation of this repulsion effect across contexts support an efficient coding mechanism in motor planning. We are now investigating how it interacts with prior-induced central tendency.

Theme 03

How we learn new motor skills

The primary distinction in motor learning lies between adaptation — adjusting a well-trained behavior to compensate for perturbations — and skill learning, which involves creating a new action–outcome mapping from scratch. Most research has focused on the former; little is known about the latter, especially at a computational level.

We revisited a longstanding paradox: patients with retrograde amnesia (e.g. HM) can learn mirror-reversed tasks, yet recent studies show the implicit system is surprisingly rigid, even after extended training.

In a recent study we trained participants for a month on novel sensorimotor maps and observed results that challenge conventional wisdom:

  1. Implicit adaptation can flip to match new mappings
  2. There is little interference between old and new maps
  3. Simple center-out reaching is more effective than complex "skill-like" tasks for inducing adaptation
  4. Mirror-reversal and rotation tasks produced similar changes in implicit adaptation

We are building neural network models to investigate the distinct roles of motor cortex and cerebellum in these processes.

Theme 04

Properties of the implicit sensorimotor recalibration system

The implicit recalibration system exhibits features unlike any other learning system. Our recent study showed that implicit recalibration does not consume attentional resources. Whether participants paid attention to the task, the feedback, or neither had no effect on learning rate — revealing the system's surprising robustness.

Learning in this system also does not require movement execution. We found that learning is maximized when visual feedback is temporally aligned with motor planning rather than with actual movement. Current work uses computational modeling to identify the driving forces behind implicit recalibration, including the role of multisensory integration.

Theme 05

The role of the cerebellum in cognition

While traditionally viewed as a motor structure, the cerebellum is increasingly implicated in cognitive domains such as reward learning and language. How it contributes to these functions is still unclear.

I conduct neurophysiological and behavioral studies with cerebellar ataxia patients and healthy controls to explore cerebellar involvement in timing, intuitive physics, social perception, and theory of mind. In particular:

  1. Timing has been considered a core cerebellar function. We focus on whether the cerebellum encodes the likelihood, the prior, or both in time perception.
  2. A domain-general hypothesis is that the cerebellum is involved in continuous operation, as in motor control and timing. I examine this using intuitive physics tasks, which require mental simulation in a continuous space.
  3. While clinical work suggests a critical cerebellar role in social perception, it is less clear which specific aspect is impaired. We examine how cerebellar patients are impaired in emotion perception, face perception, and theory of mind tasks.
